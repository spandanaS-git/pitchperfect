## Overview

The Pitch Strength Scorecard assigns a 0–100 readiness score to each of the seven opportunity types by evaluating an instructor's verified Evidence Cards against a weighted checklist of what that opportunity's decision-maker actually cares about. This document explains the rationale behind every weight assignment.

Weights were grounded in three sources:
1. **Week 1 discovery interviews** with five practitioners and buyers in ChiEAC's network (see `docs/discovery_interviews.md`).
2. **The project brief taxonomy** (Section 3.3), which was reviewed and confirmed by Dr. Benjamin M. Drury.
3. **Domain benchmarks** from the wellness and fitness industry (Yoga Alliance, IDEA Health & Fitness Association, and BLS Occupational Outlook).

Each weight is expressed as a share of 100 points. The sum of all weights per opportunity type equals exactly 100.

## 1. Studio Residency

**Target Buyer:** Studio Owner / General Manager
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Will this teacher fill a recurring slot, keep people coming back, and show up reliably?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `fill_rate_avg` | 25 | Fill rate is the single most operationally important number for a studio owner. An empty class costs the studio the room, the staff time, and the opportunity cost of a better-fit instructor. Sarah L. (Studio Manager, Week 1 interview) stated: *"I care about two things: will this instructor keep my 6:30 PM Tuesday slot full, and will new students return for class #2?"* Fill rate directly answers the first question. It receives the highest weight because no studio owner will sign a residency without evidence that the slot will be reliably attended. |
| `return_rate_30d` | 20 | Thirty-day return rate measures whether new participants come back within a month—the clearest behavioral signal that a teacher is worth sustaining. A studio's revenue model depends on converting drop-in visitors into regulars; an instructor who attracts first-timers but loses them after one class is a net cost. This metric receives the second-highest weight because it directly predicts whether a residency slot will grow or decay over time. |
| `first_to_second_conversion` | 15 | First-to-second class conversion isolates the critical first impression: did the new student like the class enough to return even once more? This is a leading indicator of long-term retention, and it surfaces problems earlier than 30-day or 90-day cohort data. It receives 15 points rather than more because it is a narrower window than the full 30-day rate; both metrics together paint a complete retention picture. |
| `classes_held_ratio` | 15 | Reliability—showing up as scheduled—is a non-negotiable baseline for any residency. A studio builds its schedule around its instructors; a late cancellation or no-show damages the studio's brand with its members. This metric (classes held vs. scheduled) receives equal weight to conversion because even a high-retention teacher who cancels frequently is an unacceptable residency partner. |
| `attendance_trend_positive` | 15 | Quarter-over-quarter attendance trend reveals whether a slot is growing, stable, or declining. A studio owner booking a new residency wants to see momentum, not just a snapshot. Positive trend signals that the instructor is actively building their following and that the slot will become more valuable over time, not less. It receives 15 points because while trend matters, a single declining quarter does not negate strong historical fill and retention numbers. |
| `unique_participant_count` | 10 | Total unique participants measures the breadth of the instructor's existing following—how many distinct people have engaged with their teaching. This matters to a studio owner because a large following reduces the risk that attendance will evaporate if a few regulars move away. It receives the lowest weight (10) because raw reach is less predictive of residency success than fill rate and return behavior, and because new instructors with a smaller but highly loyal base can still outperform those with large but disengaged followings. |

## 2. Corporate Wellness

**Target Buyer:** HR Director / People Ops / Wellness Coordinator
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Is this reliable, measurable, and easy to schedule around the workday? Will employees engage and report lower stress?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `stress_reduction_delta` | 30 | Stress reduction is the primary ROI metric corporate wellness buyers use to justify program spend to their leadership. David K. (HR Director, Week 1 interview) said: *"We get pitched by yoga teachers constantly. We reject 95% of them because they don't provide clear pricing, insurance proof, or data on stress outcomes."* The stress delta (pre-class minus post-class self-report) is the only metric in the evidence set that directly answers the HR director's accountability question: *"What did this program do for our employees?"* It receives the highest single weight in any scorecard because no corporate pitch lands without it. |
| `recommend_rate` | 20 | Employee-to-employee recommendation is the fastest driver of ongoing program participation in a workplace setting. A program that existing participants actively recommend spreads organically and reduces the HR team's ongoing marketing burden. It also signals that participation is voluntary and positive, rather than mandatory and resented—an important distinction for wellness program credibility. At 20 points it reflects that while recommendation is strong evidence, it is secondary to the direct outcome metric. |
| `overall_rating_avg` | 15 | Overall rating serves as the HR buyer's proxy for employee satisfaction and professional quality. A rating below 4.3/5.0 would create internal credibility problems for the HR director who championed the program. It receives 15 points rather than more because rating alone, without stress outcomes and recommend data, can reflect enjoyability rather than program effectiveness. |
| `lunchtime_availability` | 15 | Lunchtime and midday scheduling is a structural requirement for most corporate programs—the workday leaves limited windows for wellness activities, and lunchtime (11 AM–1 PM) is the most commonly used slot. An instructor who cannot commit to midday slots cannot serve this market, regardless of how strong their outcomes are. Equal weight to overall rating reflects that availability is a binary qualifier, not merely a nice-to-have. |
| `liability_insurance_on_file` | 10 | Liability insurance is a mandatory procurement requirement in every corporate, school, and institutional context. No HR or legal team will approve an external contractor without it. It receives 10 points rather than more because it functions as a pass/fail threshold rather than a differentiator—all viable candidates will have it; the weight penalizes instructors who do not yet have it without inflating its relative importance among outcome metrics. |
| `classes_held_ratio` | 10 | Reliability receives equal weight to insurance because corporate wellness coordinators book on fixed calendars and cannot easily reschedule if an instructor cancels. One missed session disrupts dozens of employee calendars. The 10-point weight reflects that reliability is table-stakes (most professional instructors meet it) rather than a differentiator among strong candidates. |

## 3. Community Organization

**Target Buyer:** Executive Director / Program Manager
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Is this teacher culturally responsive, accessible, affordable, and trusted by the local community?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `felt_welcomed_score` | 30 | The felt-welcomed score (Likert 1–5 on feeling included and safe in the space) is the most direct measure of whether an instructor creates an environment that is equitable and culturally responsive. Community organizations serve populations who have historically been excluded from boutique wellness spaces—if participants do not feel they belong, the program fails its mission regardless of attendance numbers. At 30 points it is the heaviest single metric in this scorecard because inclusion is the primary value proposition of a community organization partnership. |
| `repeat_attendance_share` | 20 | Repeat participation—the share of total attendance from returning participants—signals genuine community trust. In a community setting, first-time attendance can be driven by promotion; repeat attendance is driven by authentic value. An instructor who brings the same participants back regularly has built real relationships with the community, which is exactly what an executive director needs to justify the partnership to their board and funders. |
| `sliding_scale_policy` | 15 | Affordability is a structural equity requirement in community programming. An instructor who does not offer sliding-scale or community pricing cannot serve populations with limited disposable income, regardless of how high their ratings are. This policy flag receives 15 points because it determines whether the partnership is even feasible for the organization's participant base. |
| `language_matches` | 15 | Language accessibility is a direct measure of cultural reach. A Spanish-speaking instructor teaching in a predominantly Spanish-speaking community is not a bonus—it is a prerequisite for authentic service. This metric receives equal weight to sliding scale because both are structural access conditions rather than quality differentiators. |
| `quotable_community_comments` | 10 | Qualitative testimonials from consenting participants provide the human evidence that supplements quantitative scores. Executive directors use them in grant applications, board reports, and community outreach. They receive 10 points because they amplify the numerical evidence rather than replacing it—a single powerful quote does not substitute for strong belonging scores and repeat attendance. |
| `park_community_venue_history` | 10 | Teaching in parks, libraries, fieldhouses, and community centers rather than only boutique studios demonstrates that the instructor is already embedded in community infrastructure. An organization will not take a risk on an instructor who has never worked outside a private studio setting. This history receives 10 points as a signal of fit and existing community presence. |

## 4. School / Youth Program

**Target Buyer:** Principal / Athletic Director / Student Life Coordinator
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Is this instructor vetted, safe, trauma-informed, and experienced with youth in classroom or assembly settings?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `background_check_on_file` | 25 | An active, verified background check is a legal and policy requirement for any adult contractor working with minors in a school or youth setting in Illinois and most U.S. jurisdictions. Marcus T. (Youth Coach, Week 1 interview) confirmed: *"Schools require liability insurance, background checks, and proof that I can manage 25 kids safely."* This metric receives the highest weight in this scorecard because it is an absolute disqualifier if absent—no school administrator can approve a program without it, regardless of any other evidence. |
| `youth_segment_experience` | 25 | Documented experience teaching youth—flagged from class types, event types, or audience segments indicating teen, school-age, or pediatric populations—is the primary quality signal for this buyer. A technically skilled instructor who has never worked with children in a group setting represents an unacceptable program risk. Equal weight to background check reflects that safety credentials and relevant experience are co-equal requirements: one without the other is insufficient. |
| `liability_insurance_on_file` | 20 | Schools carry institutional liability for every contractor they bring on campus. General liability insurance protects both the instructor and the institution. At 20 points it receives a higher weight here than in corporate wellness (10 points) because school settings involve minors, which escalates the legal exposure and institutional scrutiny of the procurement decision. |
| `classroom_group_size_handled` | 15 | A school booking typically involves a full class, assembly, or grade-level session—30 to 150 students rather than the 10–20 typical of a boutique class. Evidence that an instructor has successfully managed large group instruction demonstrates operational readiness for the format. This metric receives 15 points because group management competency is a professional quality threshold distinct from youth experience itself. |
| `school_host_rating_avg` | 15 | Prior host ratings from educational partners (schools, youth programs, park district youth sessions) are the most credible reference signal available to a principal or athletic director. A peer institution's evaluation of the instructor's performance, reliability, and appropriateness with youth carries significant weight in procurement decisions. Equal weight to group size reflects that past institutional endorsement and current operational capacity are both required for a complete pitch. |

## 5. Festival / Large Event

**Target Buyer:** Festival Producer / Booking Agent
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Can this instructor command a large, outdoor, multi-level crowd and draw foot traffic to our stage or wellness tent?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `peak_class_size_delivered` | 30 | For a festival producer, the fundamental question is whether this instructor can fill a tent or stage. Peak class size delivered—the largest single session the instructor has run—is the most direct answer. A producer booking a wellness activation at a 5,000-person festival needs confidence that the instructor will draw 50–150 people to the wellness tent, not 12. At 30 points it is the dominant metric because it is a direct, verifiable demonstration of the instructor's crowd draw capacity. |
| `event_attendance_ratio` | 25 | The ratio of actual to expected event attendance measures whether the instructor over- or under-delivered relative to the host's projection. An instructor who consistently draws more than expected is a premium booking; one who consistently underperforms creates stage and tent dead zones that damage the festival's atmosphere. At 25 points it is the second-highest weight because it converts raw peak size into a performance track record, accounting for context and expectation. |
| `outdoor_event_track_record` | 20 | Outdoor instruction is operationally different from a studio or corporate setting—ambient noise, variable weather, uneven surfaces, and mixed audiences require specific adaptations. An instructor without outdoor event experience represents an unknown risk for a producer who is managing dozens of simultaneous logistics. This metric receives 20 points because outdoor format experience is a qualifying condition, not merely a differentiator. |
| `high_energy_comments_count` | 15 | Festival producers and booking agents respond to energy, spectacle, and crowd engagement. Consented participant comments emphasizing energy, excitement, and collective experience are qualitative evidence that the instructor can create the memorable, shareable moment a festival needs. At 15 points they supplement quantitative crowd metrics with the qualitative dimension that booking agents use to make final selections. |
| `overall_rating_avg` | 10 | Overall rating receives the lowest weight in this scorecard because festival audiences are less likely to complete structured surveys than studio regulars, and because energy and crowd draw are better predictors of festival success than aggregate satisfaction scores. It remains in the model as a professionalism floor—a rating below 4.0 would raise concerns about attendee experience even in a large-format setting. |

## 6. Hotel / Luxury Resort

**Target Buyer:** Hospitality General Manager / Guest Experience Director
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Will this elevate guest satisfaction, and is the instructor dependable for early-morning drop-in guests?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `early_morning_availability` | 25 | Hotel wellness programming—sunrise yoga, morning meditation, pre-conference stretch—is concentrated in the 6–9 AM window before guests begin their day. Elena S. (Sound Bath Guide, Week 1 interview) confirmed: *"Hotels want early-morning reliability (7 AM) and guest review snippets."* An instructor who cannot commit to early-morning slots is functionally ineligible for most hotel programming, making this metric the top qualifier at 25 points. |
| `overall_rating_avg` | 25 | Luxury hospitality is a five-star experience industry. Guest satisfaction ratings are the currency of hotel reputation management, and any programming that receives below-average feedback will be cut. A consistently high overall rating (4.7+) is both a quality signal and a risk management tool for a general manager who is accountable for every guest touchpoint. Equal weight to availability reflects that both are essential: a highly-rated instructor who cannot show up at 7 AM is as unsuitable as a reliable instructor with mediocre reviews. |
| `drop_in_experience_rate` | 20 | Hotel guests are inherently drop-in participants—they are not class regulars, they may have varying fitness levels, and they join without prior context about the instructor's style. An instructor experienced in welcoming first-time, self-selected drop-in participants can create an immediately positive experience regardless of who walks through the door. First-to-second conversion serves as a proxy for this skill, reflecting whether new participants found enough value to return even within the same trip or stay. |
| `guest_style_comments` | 15 | Qualitative feedback referencing professionalism, calm presentation, and premium experience signals alignment with the luxury hospitality brand. A hotel general manager presenting a wellness program to their ownership group needs language—*"instructor was impeccable, the room felt like a sanctuary"*—not just a numerical score. Consented testimonials at 15 points provide the narrative evidence that numbers alone cannot supply. |
| `professional_certifications` | 15 | In a luxury context, credentials serve as a brand alignment signal as much as a competency verification. An RYT-500 yoga teacher or NASM-certified instructor signals professional seriousness and reduces the liability conversation. At 15 points certifications receive a higher relative weight here than in community organization settings because the hotel buyer's brand positioning depends on presenting vetted, credentialed professionals to its guests. |

## 7. Private Event / Retreat

**Target Buyer:** Private Host / Event Planner
**Buyer's Core Question:** *"Will this experience be memorable, customized to our group, and seamless to book?"*

| Metric | Weight | Rationale |
|--------|--------|-----------|
| `repeat_booking_rate` | 30 | Repeat bookings from the same private host are the strongest possible evidence of private event success. A host who books the same instructor for their team retreat, then their holiday party, then their leadership offsite has sent an unmistakable endorsement signal. At 30 points this is the dominant metric because private event buyers are making a high-trust purchase—they are inviting an instructor into their private group—and evidence that other discerning hosts have done so repeatedly removes the largest source of buyer anxiety. |
| `host_rating_avg` | 25 | Host ratings (distinct from participant ratings) reflect the instructor's performance from the organizer's perspective: reliability, communication, customization, and professionalism. An event planner's professional reputation depends on the quality of vendors they bring in; a high host rating is the assurance they need that this instructor will not create problems on the day of the event. At 25 points it is the second-highest weight because no amount of participant enthusiasm compensates for an organizer who felt poorly served. |
| `quotable_testimonials_count` | 20 | Private event buyers frequently make decisions based on referrals and testimonials rather than metrics. A prospective host who reads a specific, enthusiastic testimonial from a comparable private client—*"our 40-person team left completely reset; we've already rebooked for next quarter"*—is substantially more likely to convert than one who sees a 4.8 star average without context. At 20 points this is the highest testimonial weight in any scorecard because qualitative social proof is uniquely powerful in the private market. |
| `private_event_experience` | 15 | Documented private event history—confirmed by the presence of event records with `event_type` matching private or retreat categories, and a minimum of two events—signals operational readiness for the format. A host hiring for a bachelorette retreat or a corporate leadership offsite needs confirmation that the instructor has navigated the logistics of private programming before. At 15 points it functions as an experience floor rather than the primary differentiator. |
| `clear_rate_card_on_file` | 10 | Event planners budget in advance and cannot close a vendor without a clear, consistent rate structure. An instructor without published rates forces the planner into an uncomfortable negotiation and signals potential unprofessionalism. At 10 points the rate card receives the lowest weight because it is a transactional prerequisite—a table-stakes qualifier—rather than a quality or outcome differentiator. Its presence in the model ensures that instructors without published pricing are penalized appropriately despite otherwise strong evidence. |

## Appendix: Score Tier Thresholds

| Tier | Score Range | Meaning |
|------|-------------|---------|
| **Pitch-Ready** | 85–100 | Evidence dossier meets or exceeds all buyer benchmarks. Ready to send a pitch today. |
| **Strong Contender** | 65–84 | Most evidence is in place. One or two gaps can be closed within 2–4 weeks. |
| **Evidence Gap** | < 65 | Material evidence is missing. The Gap Plan provides a specific collection roadmap. |

Thresholds were set to reflect realistic buyer expectations: a pitch sent with less than 65% of the required evidence signals incompleteness to the buyer and damages credibility. The 85-point Pitch-Ready threshold reflects that a small number of moderate (rather than strong) scores is acceptable—a real instructor will rarely be perfect across all dimensions—while ensuring that any pitch sent from this tool is defensible.
